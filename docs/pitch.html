Her Day

The care schedule that listens to her.

An elder's 20-second nightly voice note — in her own language — becomes the signal her care team schedules around.

Solo build ~24 hours Working demo Fictional data

The problem

Care can be flawless on paper and hollow in practice.

An elder at home is cared for, intimately and daily, by people who rotate — six in a week, agency aides turning over every few months. Each one starts from zero. What the last person learned about her left in their head.

The care record has fields for what's wrong and what's due — medications, appointments, tasks. It has no place for how she is actually doing. So the one measure that never gets taken is the one that matters most to her.

"Success" in elder care is defined by the absence of bad events — falls, missed meds, ER visits, complaints. A day with none of those counts as good, regardless of how she felt living it.

And she can't say so herself. If she is Arabic-first, has cataracts and arthritis, and has never typed on a phone, every care app on the market locks her out on the first screen. "How are you?" becomes small talk, not data — and she's learned a real answer only burdens the asker and changes nothing.

Her own experience of her days is captured nowhere, and changes nothing about how her care is arranged.

The idea

Her day, in her voice, becomes the metric care steers by.

Each evening she taps one large button and speaks for about twenty seconds, in her own language. That's the whole ask. Everything else is the system's job.

01She speaks ~20s in Arabic — "how was today, really?"
→
02Whisper transcribes & translates. Her original recording is kept, always replayable.
→
03She picks one mood and who may hear it — circle, family, coordinator, or mood only.
→
04Her check-ins trend beside the schedule — who came, what they did, time outside.
→
05The coordinator moves next week's care toward the days she felt best.

Her good days cluster around garden visits with one particular caregiver — so those visits move earlier and multiply. Remote family open the app and finally see "is she okay?" instead of a medication-adherence percentage.

Consent is enforced on the server. A note she keeps private is never sent to a hired caregiver's browser — not hidden with CSS, not transmitted at all. She owns the record; she sets who reads each line.

Mood check-ins exist elsewhere. Placing her self-reported day next to the schedule — and changing the schedule in response — does not.

Why it's different

Every tool in this space is built for the buyer.

CategoryWhat it doesThe gap
Monitoring appsFall sensors, cameras, alertsShe's a subject to be watched, never a user
Med & task appsTrack what's due and what's doneNo place for how she is
Family-coordination appsShared calendar and group chatThe elder isn't a user at all
Companion devicesChat with her, send family a summaryHer words never enter the care workflow with authority
Patient portalsAsync messaging with cliniciansUnusable at 80

None put the elder's own words into the workflow with authority. None change the schedule in response.

Built for the person usually locked out

  • Arabic-first interface with full right-to-left layout — her language is the default, not a setting
  • One large button, voice-first — nothing to type, read, or navigate
  • Read-aloud in her language for everything on screen
  • A magnifier that follows the pointer, high contrast, and real text zoom that scales the whole page
  • Consent controls she sets herself, in plain words

Built in ~24 hours, solo

A working demo, and honest about its edges.

React 19 + Vite + Tailwind v4 on the front; Node + Express + TypeScript on the back, run with tsx — no build step, no database, no native dependencies. OpenAI Whisper for transcription and translation; OpenAI TTS for read-aloud.

Demo-grade

  • All people and records are fictional
  • Shared password; tokens are forgeable
  • Pinned demo date with seeded history
  • No deployment, no database, no test suite
  • Audio is processed in the US — real use needs on-region + a privacy review

What's next

The two-way channel. When a caregiver logs a concern, or a run of hard days shows up, the app asks her one spoken question that evening — "Amina mentioned this morning was hard; do you want to tell me about it?" — and her answer routes back to the coordinator. She stops being a sensor and becomes someone the system actually asks. Then: real auth and a database, multi-family tenancy, a mobile client, native-speaker review of every string, and an accessibility audit with real elders.

The person receiving the care is the one participant who never gets asked. We made her answer the thing the system runs on.
